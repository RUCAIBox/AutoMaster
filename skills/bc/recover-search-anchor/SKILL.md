# Recover a stalled search anchor

Use this when searches keep returning the same weak matches, or a promising candidate fits one clue but conflicts with another. The aim is to obtain evidence that can change the candidate, not to accumulate more mentions of it. If a search is yielding new relevant evidence, continue it.

Before repeating a stalled query, locate its unsupported bridge. Which name, country, date, category, or interpretation did you infer, and which relation in the question actually establishes it? Keep facts attached to their subjects: a location of an affiliated organization does not establish the location of every member; an event's date does not establish a related person's tenure. A search hit containing both names does not by itself establish the requested relationship.

Choose the next action from the observed problem:

- **The candidate or scope is still a guess.** Drop that guessed restriction from the next discovery query. Search a different distinctive relation from the question that can identify an entity independently: an unusual office and succession, an event and venue, an original name and later renaming, or a study's measurable design. Use the clue's concrete terms, not every clause of the puzzle. If a new candidate appears, test the relation that the old candidate could not explain.
- **The entity is supported, but the needed fact is absent from the snippets.** Inspect the relevant page, document, or catalog entry with a focused request for that fact. An inaccessible page or a summary saying “not found” is missing evidence, not a contradiction. Seek another representation of the same document or another relevant source without discarding an otherwise supported entity.
- **A relation appears contradictory.** Check who or what each qualifier describes and whether the direction, date type, or role was reversed. Search the relation from the other endpoint if that would distinguish the interpretations. Do not silently rewrite a clue to preserve a favorite candidate.

At a consequential change, briefly connect the observation to the next action: what the results failed to establish, which assumption you are relaxing, and what the next query can distinguish. The actual query should implement that change; saying “try another approach” while retaining the same unsupported restriction is not a pivot.

After the pivot, preserve the new evidence and check the answer-bearing link. Do not return to a rejected guess merely because it is familiar. A useful alternate route may identify the target without solving every intermediate clue in order. Conversely, a popular candidate that matches only a generic clue is not established by repeated mentions. When the target and requested fact have direct support, finish; this procedure does not require an extra search, a fixed number of alternatives, or resolution of every incidental discrepancy.
