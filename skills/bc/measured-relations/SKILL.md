# Use a measurement for the relation it actually measures

Apply this when a geographic distance is being used to accept or reject a candidate. A nearby place can be a useful search lead without verifying a numerical route constraint.

Before using a returned number, identify its two endpoints, what was measured, and its units. A source's walking, driving, and straight-line distances can differ. A distance to a station is not automatically the distance to the city or institution; a short segment in directions is not the whole journey. “On the same campus,” “across the street,” an address number, and “within walking distance” establish proximity, not a specific number of kilometers. Keep that difference visible at the decision where it matters; do not add a checklist to every search.

If the missing measurement could distinguish plausible candidates, seek that relation: query the two actual place names and the requested travel mode, or fetch the relevant route/distance passage. Inspect which endpoints and mode the returned value belongs to before comparing it with the clue. Do not put the desired number into the query as an established fact. A page that lists separate road and walking distances can settle the distinction without more searches.

Unit conversion is legitimate when it preserves the same measurement. Straight-line estimates from sourced coordinates can help assess approximate geographic plausibility; label them as estimates, retain the endpoints, and do not turn them into driving or walking measurements. Do not choose coordinates, street-number scales, or rounded distances to make a digit sum or threshold fit.

If the source gives only proximity or no usable measurement, the numerical clue remains unverified. Follow an independent distinctive clue if identity is still uncertain. If other evidence already identifies the entity and supports the requested answer, finish without presenting this missing distance as confirmation. A brief honest gap is preferable to another cycle of guessing nearby places or inventing a precise match. If a measured value contradicts an answer-changing constraint, check the endpoint, mode, or candidate; keep an unresolved contradiction explicit.

When a source already supplies the required endpoints and mode, use that value and continue. This is not a requirement to measure every location or to delay a supported answer for incidental geography.
