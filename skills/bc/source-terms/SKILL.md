# Bridge the clue to the source's terms

Use this when a local biography, institution, work, or place is the likely source of a missing fact, but searches mostly return broad English summaries or repeat familiar candidates. A returned native title, local-language snippet, or established jurisdiction can indicate a better search language. A foreign-looking name alone is not enough. If the current results already answer the question, use them.

Keep one reliable anchor and the missing relation; express those in the likely source's language. Translate the relation or document type, not the whole puzzle. Preserve names, dates, and roles. Prefer a spelling or term actually returned by a source; treat a guessed translation or transliteration as a search aid, not a new fact. An unfamiliar native name can be located by pairing it with its known institution or work.

Read what changed in the results: is there a new candidate, a useful record, or the needed relation? Follow a promising record and extract that relation with its subject and date. Local-language pages can be fetched with a concise English question. Keep the native name alongside its English rendering long enough to avoid confusing people or works.

If general web search still misses an identifiable corpus, try that site's search with the anchor and local field terms. Request titles and links, then inspect a candidate record. A search hit alone does not confirm every filter. An empty or failed search does not rule out the entity.

If a short local-language probe adds no evidence, change the anchor or source instead of generating more translations. Explain the consequential switch briefly so the connection between the observed retrieval gap and the next query is visible.
